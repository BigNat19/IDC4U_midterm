1. Political conditions

A country’s economy is only as steady as its politics. Things to check:

  • Depends on the leader
  • Depends on the party
  • Conflicts
  • Type of government (e.g. communist)
  • Geographical area (e.g. trade)

2. Balance of payments

DefinitionHow money comes into and out of a country (flow of money, in × out).
  • Nations take in debt that will return. That’s ROI, return on investment.

3. Economic indicators: interest rates

Good numberUnder 5%.

Interest is the cost of borrowing. Lower rates make loans like mortgages cheaper (see the example at the bottom).

Inflation

DefinitionAn overall rise in prices.
  • Canada’s target inflation rate is 2%.
  • The central bank monitors the economy.

Deflation

DefinitionHappens when people don’t have enough disposable income.
Deflation is also bad. It shows a weak economy.

GDP per capita and PPP

GDPThe total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
GDP per capitaGDP ÷ population: the average economic output per person.
PPP (purchasing power parity)Adjusts for cost of living using a “basket of goods” (food, housing, etc.). Poorer countries often look better off than at simple exchange rates.

GDP vs GNI

GNIGDP + money citizens earn abroad − money foreign companies take out (includes remittances and profits earned overseas).
Ireland: lots of foreign corporations (tech, pharma) and profits leave, so GDP > GNI. It looks richer than people feel.

Why GDP isn’t enough

  • It counts every transaction, good or bad.
  • Robert Kennedy: GNP measures everything except what makes life worthwhile.
  • Diminishing returns: happiness gains flatten out around $80–100k.
  • Growing GDP is most useful for very poor countries.

Human Development Index (HDI)

  • Wealth (GNI per capita) · Health (life expectancy) · Education (years of schooling)
  • Equatorial Guinea: GDP per capita over $19,000 in 2008, but life expectancy 63.7 and 8.3 mean years of schooling. High GDP doesn’t guarantee high HDI.

The resource curse (paradox of plenty)

DefinitionCountries with abundant resources (oil, gas, minerals) get slower growth, weak institutions and more conflict.
  • Dutch disease: resource exports raise the currency, making other exports less competitive
  • Corruption and weak institutions
  • Conflict over control of resources
  • Inequality: a small elite benefits

Examples: Nigeria (Niger Delta), Venezuela, DR Congo. Avoided it: Norway, Canada, Botswana.

Inequality, Gini and friends

Inequality here means the income gap within a country. Averages hide it (“feet in the fire, head on ice: on average just fine”).

  • Gini coefficient: 0 = perfect equality, 100 (or 1) = perfect inequality. About 25 for a Scandinavian country, about 50 for a Sub-Saharan one.
  • Rough guide: 0.25–0.35 more equal · 0.35–0.45 moderate · 0.45+ more concerning
  • Lorenz curve: the further it sags from the equality line, the higher the Gini.
  • Quintile ratio: average income of richest 20% ÷ poorest 20%.
  • Top 1% share: Finland 7.3%, Canada 13.6%, Brazil 28.4%.
  • Gini measures income, not wealth, and ignores regional differences.
  • Rawls’ veil of ignorance: design a fair society without knowing who you’ll be.

Gender Inequality Index (GII)

  • Adolescent birth rate
  • Share of seats in parliament held by women
  • Labour force participation (males vs females)

PHDI (Planetary pressures-adjusted HDI)

HDI adjusted for CO₂ emissions per capita and material footprint per capita, because development shouldn’t wreck the planet.

Demographic indicators

  • Crude birth rate: live births per 1,000 people per year
  • Crude death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year
  • Natural increase rate: (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10, as a %. Example: (30 − 10) ÷ 10 = 2.0%.
When you see a trend, ask why. Example: Afghanistan’s labour force participation by sex.

Measuring happiness

  • Eudaimonia (Aristotle): flourishing or living well
  • Affective wellbeing: are you happy? Evaluative wellbeing: are you living a good life?
  • Cantril ladder: 0 (worst possible life) to 10 (best possible life), now and in five years
  • World Happiness Report factors: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom to make life choices, generosity (donations), perceptions of corruption

Economic indicators

How do we measure the strength of an economy?

Step 1: evaluate the strength of their currency using the three areas below.

1. Political conditions

  • Depends on the leader
  • Depends on the party
  • Conflicts
  • Type of government (e.g. communist)
  • Geographical area (e.g. trade)

2. Balance of payments

  • How money comes into and out of a country (flow of money, in × out)
  • Nations take in debt that will return (ROI, return on investment)

3. Economic indicators

  • Interest rates: a good number is under 5%
  • Inflation: an overall rise in prices. Canada’s target is 2%.
  • Deflation: happens when people don’t have enough disposable income. Also bad; shows a weak economy.
  • Unemployment rate: a good number is under 10%
  • Balance of trade: imports vs exports. You want more exports (trade surplus); less would be a trade deficit.
  • Oil prices
  • GDP: the value of all goods and services produced in a nation. GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population (compare to other nations).
  • Consumer Price Index (CPI): a basket of goods to get a feel for the price of goods in a nation
  • Level of debt: is it manageable? (check BP)

Beyond the economy

Economic development

GDP, GDP per capita, PPP

  • GDP: total value of goods and services produced in a country in a year.
  • GDP per capita: GDP ÷ population, the average output per person.
  • PPP: adjusts for cost of living using a “basket of goods,” so poorer countries look better off than at market exchange rates.
  • Income groups: low under $1,035 · middle $1,035 to $12,615 · high $12,616+

GDP vs GNI

GNI = GDP + money citizens earn abroad − money foreign companies take out. Ireland has a high GDP per capita because of foreign corporations, but profits leave, so GDP > GNI and it looks richer than people feel.

Why GDP isn’t enough

It counts every transaction, good or bad. Robert Kennedy said it measures everything except what makes life worthwhile. Happiness gains flatten out around $80–100k (diminishing returns). GDP growth still matters for very poor countries.

Human development

HDI

Three parts: health (life expectancy), education (years of schooling), wealth (GNI per capita). Equatorial Guinea had GDP per capita above $19,000 in 2008 but life expectancy of 63.7 and 8.3 mean years of schooling.

The resource curse (paradox of plenty)

Resource-rich countries often get slower growth, weak institutions and more conflict.

  • Dutch disease: resource exports raise the currency, hurting other exports
  • Corruption and weak institutions
  • Conflict over control of resources
  • Inequality: a small elite benefits

Examples: Nigeria, Venezuela, DR Congo. Avoided it: Norway, Canada, Botswana.

Inequality

Measures

  • Inequality: income gap within a country.
  • Gini coefficient: 0 = perfect equality, 100 (or 1) = perfect inequality. About 0.25–0.35 more equal, 0.35–0.45 moderate, 0.45+ concerning.
  • Lorenz curve: the more it sags from the equality line, the higher the Gini.
  • Quintile ratio: average income of richest 20% ÷ poorest 20%.
  • Top 1% share: Finland 7.3%, Canada 13.6%, Brazil 28.4%.
  • Gini measures income, not wealth, and ignores regional differences.

Adjusted indexes

  • IHDI: HDI after losses from inequality. Loss = (HDI − IHDI) ÷ HDI. Example: 0.786 → 0.594 is a 24.4% loss.
  • GII: adolescent birth rate, seats in parliament held by women, labour participation.
  • PHDI: HDI adjusted for CO₂ emissions and material footprint per person.
  • Rawls’ veil of ignorance: design a society without knowing who you’ll be.

Demographics and happiness

Demographic indicators

  • Crude birth rate: births per 1,000 per year
  • Crude death rate: deaths per 1,000 per year
  • Natural increase rate: (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10, as a %

With any trend, ask WHY it happened.

Measuring happiness

  • Eudaimonia (Aristotle): flourishing, living well.
  • Affective wellbeing: are you happy? Evaluative: are you living a good life?
  • Cantril ladder: 0 to 10 rating of your life now and in five years.
  • World Happiness Report factors: GDP per capita, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, generosity, perceptions of corruption.
